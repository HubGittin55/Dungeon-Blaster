The game in one page

Dungeon Blaster is a round-based fantasy RPG for up to four players on a single Windows 11 laptop
(couch co-op: one screen, one mic, one set of speakers). Each player speaks or types what they want to do.
A large language model (LLM) plays the Dungeon Master (DM): narrating, inventing, ruling on attempts, and
voicing its own lines. A diffusion model draws what the DM describes. A deterministic tracker, not the DM,
owns the facts: inventory, materials, levels, experience, and what has already been generated.

The signature mechanic is open naming with a closed gate. A player can name an item within reason and
add it to their inventory, provided they hold the crafting materials to make it. The image model then
generates the item's art and the system assigns its stats in real time, inside a budget set by fixed material
and item tiers tied to character level.

   The core design tension, kept on purpose: the DM has a super wacky, sacked-out demeanor. He
   forgets, improvises, and rules by vibes. The tracker is sober. The DM is the unreliable narrator; the
   tracker is the source of truth. Comedy comes from the gap between the two, never from the records
   being wrong.

Design pillars

   Name anything, pay for everything. Creativity is free; power costs materials.
   Tiers are law. Material and item tiers are fixed to levels. The DM cannot talk his way past the table.
   Generated once, kept forever. Items, characters, and environments are generated on demand, then
   cached as permanent game assets.
   Voice first. Characters are created by speaking into a microphone. Narration is spoken back in the DM's
   voice.
   Local only, modest hardware. The whole kit runs on the constraining laptop defined in this document.
   No cloud calls in the core loop.
   Four players, one table. Up to four players share the laptop. Everything on screen is public.
   Identity is a slot, not an account. Slots 1-4 own characters. Input is attributed by push-to-talk key or
   typed slot, never by anything the DM says.
   Say it only after it's checked. Nothing is voiced until the tracker gate has passed the mutations it
   describes.

Status

including seconds per generated image on the laptop, are unmeasured until the first vertical slice runs.

Expected DM speed on this laptop

The reference band for the chosen DM model on a desktop Ryzen 9700X with DDR5-6400 is 18 to 25
tokens per second. The laptop should reach at least 85 percent of that: memory frequency is 87.5 percent of
the desktop (5600 / 6400), channel count is identical, the clock and architecture gaps barely matter for a
bandwidth-bound workload, and 80 W is ample power for it. That puts the laptop band at roughly 15 to 21
tokens per second, with about 20 tokens per second a realistic in-band expectation rather than a stretch.

Decode speed is not the number players feel. Narration streams to TTS in sentence chunks, and spoken
English runs ~3-4 tokens/s (estimate) against 15-21 tok/s generation, so after the first sentence playback,
not compute, is the bottleneck. The metric that matters is time-to-first-audio: STT clip + prefill of the new
tokens (prefix cache warm) + ~20 tokens of decode + first TTS chunk. Prefill at 12K+ tokens on this CPU is
unmeasured and is the likeliest source of felt latency (see the DM context budget). Narration length is
budgeted in audio seconds, not compute.

Rule for all performance claims: laptop figures in this document are planning estimates derived as
above. Each one gets replaced by a measured figure during the first slice, and the estimate is then
deleted, not kept beside it.

Four runtimes, one bridge

The game client is a presentation and input layer only. It renders the scene, the inventory, and the character
sheet; it captures typed and spoken input; it plays audio. It contains no game rules and it never talks to the
model servers or to the Model Context Protocol (MCP) directly.

Pi, the agent harness, is the only bridge. Pi receives player intent from the client, retrieves the relevant
rules and current state, prompts the DM, validates every proposed effect through the tracker, dispatches
speech and image jobs, and returns a single resolved turn to the client. This mirrors the proven local pattern:
Pi treats a local OpenAI-compatible endpoint as a first-class provider, and Pi extensions have already driven
a running ComfyUI server through Comfy MCP to produce game assets.

The DM brain: one unified model

One model does three jobs: reasoning as DM, calling tools, and writing the lines it will speak. Keeping all
three in one context is deliberate. The voice and the logic cannot drift apart, the persona stays consistent
between a ruling and its narration, and there is no second model to load, schedule, or keep in character.

Primary pick: Qwen3.5-35B-A3B at Q4 / IQ4_XS, on CPU

This is a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model: 35 billion total parameters, only about 3 billion active per token.
CPU decoding is bandwidth-bound, so the model that streams fewer active parameters per token wins. The
35B MoE streams roughly 3B worth of weights per token; a dense 9B streams all 9B. The larger model is
therefore both smarter and faster on CPU, and it costs RAM rather than speed.

Fallback: Qwen 3.5 9B at Q5

If the 35B proves too heavy in practice, the dense 9B at Q5 (about 6.2 GB) is the drop-in fallback. It is the
stronger tool caller of the small options considered, it fits even on the GPU if placement ever changes, and
its 262K context holds the rules document, inventory, and session history together. The alternative
considered, Gemma 4 12B at Q4, was rejected on fit and on tool calling: about 7.3 GB of weights and
roughly 8.8 to 9 GB total at 32K context exceeds the 8 GB card, and its agentic scores trail the Qwen
options.

Model identity note: Qwen3.5-35B-A3B is a distinct model from Qwen3.6-35B-A3B. They share a shape and CPU math; they
are not interchangeable names in configs or logs.

The cache key is the stable entity ID. Prompt, seed, model, and appearance hash live in Asset
metadata, not in the key, so re-describing an item cannot collide or trigger new art. Only engine events
generate art (item created, location first entered, NPC introduced, creature type first met). A re-roll creates a
new asset version pointing at the same entity. Stacks share art.

Async by default. A round never waits on the GPU. Placeholder art is the normal first state, and an
asset_ready event swaps in the real image. GPU queue priority: TTS > scene backdrop > item art >
portraits.

The image model: Anima

Anima is a 2-billion-parameter model from CircleStone Labs and Comfy Org, built on Cosmos-Predict2, for
anime and illustration rather than photorealism. That style suits a fantasy game and stays consistent across
items, people, and places. The full stack is about 5.6 GB (4.2 GB model, 1.2 GB text encoder, plus the
variational autoencoder), so it fits the 8 GB card at full precision, and an INT8 ConvRot build exists for a
smaller footprint, matching the established INT8-first policy for diffusion on these cards.

Sampling uses the official Turbo configuration of 8 to 12 steps at CFG 1. Experimental 4-step merges exist
but carry poor diversity and a not-recommended status; they are not part of this design. At 8 steps,
generation is seconds per image on this class of GPU, which fits turn-based pacing. Exact seconds per
image on the target laptop are unmeasured and are a first-slice benchmark, not a promise.

License: Anima's weights are under a non-commercial license. That is fine for a personal kit. Because
every asset is generated at runtime through a workflow, swapping the image model later is a workflow
change, not a content rebuild. If Dungeon Blaster ever ships commercially, the image model is replaced
at that point and existing cached art is regenerated or relicensed.

Table rules: four players, one laptop

Four players share one screen, one mic, and one set of speakers. These rules give the authority boundary
per-actor teeth, so one player's prompt cannot spend another player's inventory.

   Slots. Slots 1-4, each owning one character. An empty slot owes no intent. Input is attributed by PTT key
   or typed slot, never by DM text.
   Rounds. COLLECTING builds one editable intent card per slot; the round resolves when all active slots are
   ready or the AFK timer auto-holds the rest. One resolve_round call per round. Combat resolves a full
   initiative cycle per call; per-actor calls are allowed if they feel better (tuning, not architecture).
   Intent-bound mutations. A mutation touching a slot's inventory or stats must cite an intent from that slot
   this round. World- and NPC-scoped mutations need no intent. Anything without a valid actor/intent is
   rejected.
   Transfers. transfer_item also needs the owner's confirm keypress on the shared screen.
   Contention. Two slots claiming the same loot or materials resolve in deterministic order (initiative, then
   seeded by round). The seed is logged in TurnLog . The DM may flavor the outcome, never choose it.
   Crafting gate. The level check applies to the crafter; the item's owner may be a different slot (open; see
   open decisions).
   Names are data. Free-text names are length-limited and fenced as data in the prompt. The engine
   ignores instructions inside them.
   One public screen. All state is visible to the table. Per-player inventory is a per-slot toggle, not a secret.
   No hidden rolls.
   Character creation pipelines. Slot k records while the DM clamps slot k-1's sheet. Portraits queue in the
   background and play starts on placeholders.

Say your dream character out loud

Character creation is the showcase flow for the whole stack, and it runs in the first minutes of the game.

Sequence

   1. Speak. The player describes their dream character into the microphone, in their own words, at
   whatever length they like.
   2. Transcribe. audio.cpp turns the speech into text. The player can correct the transcript before anything
   is built from it.
   3. Split. The DM produces two artifacts from one description: a character sheet (class, attributes, starting
   kit, hooks) and an image prompt (appearance, gear, mood, composition).
   4. Clamp. The sheet passes through the rules document and the tracker. Claims beyond the level 1
   budget are converted, not granted: "I am a god" returns as a level 1 character with divine confidence, a
   dramatic hat, and a fixed stat budget. Flavor survives; power does not leak.
   5. Draw. Pi sends the image prompt through Comfy MCP to Anima. The portrait is cached against the
   new character ID.
   6. Introduce. The DM voices the introduction in character, the portrait and sheet appear together, and the
   player confirms or asks for one portrait re-roll.

   Design rule: re-rolls change the picture, never the stats. Re-rolling a portrait must not become a slot
   machine for a stronger character, so the sheet is locked before the first image is drawn.

Failure handling

If transcription is garbled, the player edits text instead of repeating themselves. If image generation fails or
times out, the character is created anyway with a placeholder silhouette and the portrait is queued for retry;
a missing picture never blocks play.

The DM proposes, the tracker disposes

Rules as a retrieved document (RAG)

All hard rules live in one versioned rules document: tier tables, crafting costs, stat budgets, progression
curves, and naming limits. On every turn, Pi retrieves the relevant sections and places them in the DM's
context alongside current state. This is retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) used as law, not as lore. The
DM rules against the document in front of it, and editing the document is how the game is balanced. No
code change is needed to retune a tier.

Inventory and progression are data, not narration

Crafting check, in order

   The player names an item and describes it. The DM interprets the request and proposes an item type and
   tier.
   The tracker checks level against the tier gate. Too early means no, with the unlock level stated.
   The tracker checks materials: required types and quantities must be in inventory. Missing materials are
   named exactly.
   The engine deducts materials, computes stats inside the budget, creates the item record, and requests its
   art.
   The DM narrates the result, in voice, with the generated portrait of the item on screen.

Progression

Experience, level, unlocked tiers, known locations, and quest state are tracker fields, updated by validated
events only. The DM can award experience as a tool call; the tracker applies the curve from the rules
document and announces level-ups as state changes, so a generous mood cannot quietly inflate a
character. Saves are snapshots of tracker state plus the asset cache index, which makes a save file small,
inspectable, and portable.

Open decisions for the next planning pass

   Final tier names, level thresholds, and stat-budget numbers inside the rules document structure.
   Portrait re-roll allowance per character and per item (currently: one at creation, stats locked).
   Whether environments get one backdrop per location or a small set of variants per location state.
   Save format packaging: tracker snapshot plus asset folder, zipped per campaign.
   Multiplayer scope. Decided: local couch co-op, 1-4 players on one laptop, from Slice 1 (see Table rules).
   Networked play stays out of scope. Phones as private controllers is a later idea and would reopen
   transport, HTTPS, and auth.
   Tracker store: proposed SQLite alone: an append-only events table plus materialized state tables, WAL
   mode, JSONL export for debugging. Justification is replay-debugging and tidy saves, not reconnects.
   Save location still open.
   Rules retrieval: proposed deterministic section/tag selection from this round's intents, no embeddings.
   Log section IDs + content hash per round. The engine computes stats, so the DM only needs to see what
   it may propose.
   Asset serving: same local server and channel as rounds.
   Tool-call schema: decided shape is one resolve_round call (see Prompt contract). Still needs a written
    docs/tools.md before Slice 1, with actor + intent on every mutation.
   New, open: shared party XP vs individual levels; crafting gate on crafter vs owner; combat batched per
   initiative cycle vs per actor.

Build order: vertical slices

   Slice 1 - Harness and law: Pi plus llama-server plus rules document plus tracker. Four slots, text-only
   rounds, validated crafting, no voice, no art. Four scripted bot slots (one deliberately evil: duplicate crafts,
   loot races, impersonation, hostile item names) play a 10-round scene against the real tracker with
   deterministic contention. resolve_round mid-stream gating verified, or the two-pass fallback adopted.
   TTFT at 12K recorded.
   Slice 2 - Voice: audio.cpp in and out. Spoken actions, spoken narration, voice character creation with a
   text sheet.
   Slice 3 - Art: Comfy MCP plus Anima. Item art first, then portraits, then environments, all cached.
   Slice 4 - The full loop: progression, saves, level-ups, tier unlocks, and a complete first dungeon run on
   the laptop, benchmarked.

Dungeon Blaster earns its name the same way its items are made: named freely, built from what is actually
on hand, and checked against the rules before it counts.
